A value proposition connects a particular customer's important task with a result your offer can credibly help deliver. It is more specific than a mission and more useful than a feature inventory. It should help the buyer decide whether an evaluation is worthwhile.

## Define whose value matters

Use this guide when positioning is reasonably clear but the offer still sounds interchangeable. Start with one segment, one buying situation and one role. A user may value less repetitive work while a budget owner needs predictable operating costs. Develop the connection between those outcomes instead of forcing both people into one generic promise.

## Build the proposition from evidence

1. Describe the task and the current alternative. Use examples from recent customer work. Separate the person's stated problem from your interpretation of its commercial significance.
2. Rank the important consequences. Ask which inconvenience is tolerable and which failure prompts action. Time saved is not automatically money saved: identify what changes with the released capacity.
3. Explain the product mechanism. Name the capability that creates the result, what the customer must supply, and the conditions under which it works. “Uses AI” does not explain why a particular outcome improves.
4. Select evidence matched to the promise. A successful demonstration can establish capability under stated conditions. A measured customer result can support a narrower outcome claim. Neither automatically supports every buyer or deployment.
5. State the meaningful tradeoff. Identify setup effort, required integrations, workflow change or a category of customer you do not yet serve. Let a poor-fit buyer recognize the limitation before a sales call.
6. Test comprehension and commitment separately. Ask a reader to explain who the offer is for and what changes. Then observe a relevant action, such as providing a sample for evaluation. A memorable line and a credible purchase case are different tests.

## Worked example

A fictional invoice product begins with “Automate finance with intelligent workflows.” Its proposed replacement is: “For finance teams reconciling supplier invoices across multiple warehouses, bring mismatches into one review queue with the source record attached.”

The claim describes a mechanism and task. To add “cut reconciliation time by half,” the team would need evidence with a defined baseline, workload, measurement window and customer conditions. Until then, the next step is a timed evaluation on a representative batch, including difficult exceptions rather than only clean invoices.

## A brief to use before writing copy

```text
Audience and current situation:
Task and current alternative:
Consequence the buyer cares about:
Capability that changes the work:
Customer prerequisites:
Proof available and its limits:
Tradeoff or exclusion:
Proposed statement:
Comprehension test and evaluation task:
```

## Review the promise

Keep a record of misunderstandings, not just preferred wording. If readers infer a capability you do not provide, revise the language even when the sentence is technically defensible. If the claimed benefit is attractive but nobody will attempt the evaluation, investigate urgency, trust and switching effort. Do not solve every objection by making the promise larger.
