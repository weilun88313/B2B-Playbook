**Last reviewed:** 2026-08-29

Nearly half the B2B companies in one later-successful interview set went through at least one failed idea first. Some went through ten. Christina Cacioppo’s version: the first couple of ideas were total crap. Retool was Venmo for the U.K. Amplitude was voice-to-text. Segment was a classroom lecture tool. Vanta was B2B Alexa. Notion was a no-code website builder. Loom was a marketplace for hiring experts. Slack was a game. Box was a Facebook photo box.

The question is not “do we have a slogan.” It is: **is this a real idea, or more crap?** This chapter sits after [idea discovery](idea-discovery.md) and before [ICP](icp.md). You are still allowed to be wrong about *who*. You are not allowed to go all-in on polite interest.

All four paths below are different investments in the same two things: **pain** (the problem matters) and **pull** (they actually want *your* way). Product-market fit—love, pay, pull at a growing scale—is a later ladder: [PMF](product-market-fit.md).

## Use this when

- The team has an idea and is arguing about whether to quit jobs, raise, or write code.
- Founders are treating friends, batch-mates, or investor intros as proof.
- Conversations sound like “that’s cool, I might buy” and nobody has cursed the incumbent.
- A prototype exists and nobody can say what would make them kill the idea this week.

## Do not use this when

- You do not have a candidate yet—no path, no three ingredients. That is [idea discovery](idea-discovery.md).
- You already have dated evidence of pay, usage, emotion, or cold inbound and are choosing *who* to serve. That is [ICP](icp.md).
- You are hunting the first ten logos. That is [first ten customers](first-ten-customers.md).
- You want a metric that replaces talking to people. There isn’t one.

## What actually counts as legs

Stop when you see these—not when the calendar says you “did customer development”:

| Signal | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Money** | Several people pay, or offer to, ideally with **no** personal tie |
| **Usage** | They keep using a hacky prototype |
| **Emotion** | Hatred of the incumbent, or a strong reaction to *your* idea—not “neat mint, organic, local, maybe I’d buy” |
| **Cold inbound** | Strangers find you |

**Cold outbound** is consistently the cleanest test in that sample—purer than friends, incubator classmates, or investor leads. Retool and Zip both treated selling to people who owe you as a way to fool yourself. DoorDash as Retool’s fifth customer came from cold sales.

Only about a third of that set used design partners. Do not copy a partner program because it sounds mature.

Median conversations before the idea felt solid: **about 30**. Zip did ~75 in two to three weeks. Ramp did 100+ before a card shipped. Amplitude did 30 in a month, then built; **zero** of those thirty became paid customers. Spenser Skates later wished for 50, and for keeping half his time talking after they started building.

## Pick one path

Match the path to how sure you are, not to what sounds founderly.

### Path 1: do it by hand (problem unclear or maybe unsolvable)

Build nothing. Do the job manually for a handful of companies. Least common in the sample; cheapest learning when it works.

**Vanta:** six months, ~24 companies, before code. Christina answered security questionnaires by copy-paste, read two dozen SOC 2 reports, ran a readiness assessment, and made a spreadsheet “report card.” The tests: would they spend time, and would they *believe* the spreadsheet? Segment liked it. Find-and-replace “Segment” → “Front” still helped Front—so it might standardize. An old Dropbox colleague emailed: I hear you are SOC 2 consultants, come do us. That was when they started writing code.

**Ramp:** “savings reports” from 90 days of card and ACH data, returned as if software. One company was paying ~$100k for abandoned project-management tools. The gift of $200k in savings *without* requiring Ramp was the aha—and the pitch for what the product would do automatically.

### Path 2: listen first (default unless you already know what to build)

Talk until pain and pull show up, **then** build. Aim near 30; do not stop at a round number.

**Zip:** ~75 CFOs / procurement / finance in two to three weeks, 110 pages of notes, LinkedIn asking for *advice*. Three ideas; two killed; one honed daily. A 16-point idea checklist, and the honesty that **today is the easiest day to kill it**.

**Gusto:** 30 people before a line of code, including friends-of-friends and names off Yelp. Call 1 was exploratory; call 30 was mostly confirmation. What they wanted was emotion: more than half **cursed** their payroll provider. “Yeah, that’s cool, I might buy” means they will not.

**Stytch:** ~30 people, mostly fintech developers; almost everyone hated Auth0, Firebase, Cognito, or homegrown. Snowball of inevitability. They **skipped** design partners, shipped self-serve magic links, and followed investor advice: a large company will keep asking for what you cannot build yet; serve the long tail first.

**Snyk:** the security-in-dev problem was already obvious. Guy Podjarny talked to dozens of **developers and DevOps founders**—not security buyers—to see if they would *embrace* a tool. DevOps people were supportive; the few security people were skeptical it was possible. That gap was conviction: hard, and big if it works.

**Ramp** again: 100+ finance and founder teams (YC, friends, New York) *before* the manual reports.

If the calls stay polite, you do not have an idea yet. Keep going, or kill it.

### Path 3: prototype with a few design partners (you already know the space)

Build something you would use. Co-create with a **small** set. For founders with scars in the category: Gong, Retool, Linear, Sprig, Zip, Hex.

**Retool:** first version for themselves; then tiny YC companies that already had PMF and needed internal tools (~5 of 200). The tell was **usage**, then cold outbound so they could not delude themselves.

**Linear:** weekends, for themselves, then 2–5 person startups. Requests from larger customers parked. Friends split binary: “I hate product X” vs “GitHub Issues is fine.” Serve the people in pain; you will not convert the others by arguing.

**Gong:** 12 design partners, software in January, charge in May, **11 of 12 bought**. Eilon Reshef watched session recordings like a maniac (~two hours a day) and called when stuck—what they *did*, not what they said.

**Sprig:** four companies of similar scale (Plann, Thunkable, Square, Robinhood) so the roadmap did not fork. The real test: could cold email get a meeting with someone who then *used* it? Thunkable: demo, install, come back a week later, then price. First customer with no prior tie.

**Hex:** two design partners—“the minimum that’s more than one.” One started using it for real and asking for features. Don’t dilute across a crowd. Same lesson as the [PMF](product-market-fit.md) one-company obsession.

### Path 4: just launch (desperate, or the vision is already sharp)

**Segment:** nine months of design-partner building; overlap with actual new users was near zero. Calvin French-Owen: users find products better than founders find partners—especially with no network. They launched because they were out of money. Backup idea (group trip planning) would have been a disaster.

**Loom:** Product Hunt, thousands of extension downloads by end of day; every signup got a founder welcome email; founders did support for a year.

**Canva:** ~70% vision, 30% feedback; prototypes in front of people, then production user-testing for onboarding and communication—not to discover the category.

**Databricks:** open source already had thousands of users before it was a company. Mindshare without a business is a different starting line.

Prosumer collaboration tools in that telling (Figma, Notion, Coda, Airtable, Miro, Slack) often **wandered three to four years** before something clicked. Notion rebuilt in Kyoto on a shoestring after money ran out. Figma spent years in stealth before a private beta. Coda’s 1.0 took four and a half years on purpose. Airtable’s “simmer”: years of prototype before rush. That is not a license to hide. It is a warning that *this* category of product is slow—and that “we launched” is not validation unless the market answers.

## When to go all-in

Pain and pull are the bar. Founders also named a *moment*. None of these replace the table above.

| Pattern | What it was in that sample |
|---|---|
| The right people say yes | Coda: if the first four hires all join, we start |
| One conversation that will not leave your head | Gusto: accountant ranted for 30 minutes before they pitched; parking-lot “we’re doing payroll.” Census: an operator with no process, then an Amazon friend whose “Rolls-Royce” pipeline lost leads |
| Momentum until it is not a decision | Stytch’s snowball; Front’s landing-page signups plus calls |
| They give you time | Vanta: Segment let them sit with engineering as “SOC 2 consultants” |
| Desperation | Segment launching because real jobs were weeks away |
| The universe moves you | Hex: both founders had to relocate anyway |
| You were always going to start *something* | Figma after the Thiel Fellowship; Amplitude’s two-year no-quit rule; Snyk incorporated eight days after leaving Akamai |

Census called the Amazon conversation **founder-motivation fit**, not PMF. Gusto committed to *each other* before the idea. Do not skip the team test.

## Copyable templates

### How a filled validation card reads

Teaching fill: a founder who used to drown in security questionnaires, not sure the pain is real.

| Field | Fill |
|---|---|
| Path this month | Manual (Vanta-shaped): do three questionnaires by hand |
| Conversations so far | 12 / target ~30 |
| Polite “that’s cool” | 9 |
| Cursed the incumbent / grabbed the spreadsheet | 2 |
| Paid or offered to pay (no personal tie) | 0 |
| Cold inbound | 1 (old colleague heard we “consult”) |
| Kill rule | If call 30 is still mint-and-maybe, we stop |

Highest honest signal here: **one stranger asked for the manual work.** Not an idea yet. Keep the path or change it.

## Pre-flight checklist

- [ ] We named a path; we are not doing all four as a personality.
- [ ] “Validation” includes at least one cold signal or a dated reason we cannot get one yet.
- [ ] Polite interest is logged as polite, not as pull.
- [ ] Friends and batch-mates are labeled as contaminated tests unless we are explicitly on the Coda “they’ll quit if it’s bad” experiment.
- [ ] Design-partner count is small on purpose, or we wrote why we skipped partners (Stytch-shaped).
- [ ] If we already built, half the week is still talking—Amplitude’s miss.
- [ ] Going all-in is tied to pain/pull or a named pattern, not a tweet thread.
- [ ] Failed previous ideas are on paper so we remember we can kill this one too.

## Metrics

| Metric | Diagnostic use |
|---|---|
| Conversations with emotion or money | Not total calls |
| Cold vs warm among people who pay or use | Purity of the test |
| Kill speed | Days from “meh” to a written kill—Zip’s easiest-day rule |
| Partner conversion | Design partners who buy when you charge (Gong-shaped) |
| Overlap | Design partners vs actual users after launch (Segment-shaped) |

Do not count a completed interview sprint, a landing page, or “we’re in stealth.”

## Common mistakes

- Building after 30 polite calls (Amplitude’s trap).
- Treating YC classmates as the market (Retool, Zip, Sprig).
- Collecting design partners until you are mediocre for everyone (Hex).
- Building for one enterprise that will delay you a year (Stytch / Benchmark advice).
- Launching as avoidance when the problem is still mush—unless you are actually out of time.
- Staying in stealth for years on a collaboration tool and calling it taste.
- Confusing founder-motivation fit with [PMF](product-market-fit.md).

## What to read next

If you still do not have a candidate, go back to [idea discovery](idea-discovery.md). If pain and pull are real, name **who**: [ICP](icp.md), then [buying committee](buying-committee.md). Then [find the first ten](first-ten-customers.md)—warm rings and targeted cold, which is also how you keep validating. Whether they love it and pay at a growing scale is [product-market fit](product-market-fit.md). Do not open [channel strategy](../04-channels-and-distribution/channel-strategy.md) to save a mushy idea.

## Sources and evidence boundary

This is an owner-maintained operating synthesis. It will not tell you whether *your* idea is good.

The ~40% pivot rate (vs ~20% B2C in that comparison), the four paths, the four pull signs, the ~30-conversation median, outbound as the cleanest signal, the ~one-third design-partner share, the prosumer 3–4 year wander, and the go-all-in patterns draw on Lenny Rachitsky’s founder interviews ([Lenny’s Newsletter, 2023-08-15](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-validate-your-b2b-startup?ref=b2b-playbook)). Named episodes (Vanta, Ramp, Zip, Stytch, Gusto, Snyk, Amplitude, Retool, Linear, Gong, Sprig, Hex, Segment, Loom, Canva, Databricks, Notion, Figma, Coda, Airtable, Census, Front, and the failed first ideas listed at the top) are that author’s illustrations, not audited case studies here. Zip’s 16-point checklist is in the source; do not treat it as this repository’s idea scorecard.
