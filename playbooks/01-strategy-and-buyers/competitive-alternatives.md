The most important competitor may be the approach a buyer already uses. An internal analyst, an agency, a spreadsheet or postponing the project can be a rational choice. Positioning starts to improve when these alternatives become concrete.

## Investigate the actual choice

Use this guide before writing a comparison page or deciding which differences to emphasize in a sales conversation. Focus on one buying situation. A list of companies with similar websites does not establish what the buyer will compare.

## Build an alternatives map

1. Ask what the buyer would do if your product were unavailable. Follow up with how that approach works today, who maintains it and what it costs in effort as well as money. Avoid suggesting a rival name before hearing the answer.
2. Separate the current approach from options actively evaluated. Record evidence and date. A salesperson's guess is not the same as a buyer naming a shortlist.
3. List the buyer's selection criteria and constraints. Include implementation effort, control, reliability, existing relationships and consequences of failure. Do not score every alternative only against your strongest feature.
4. Compare mechanisms and tradeoffs. Explain what each approach makes possible and where it becomes difficult. Verify competitor product statements against current official material; label unknowns instead of inventing disadvantages.
5. Identify the reason to change now. A better feature is insufficient if migration risk and organizational effort overwhelm the expected benefit. State the condition under which remaining with the old approach is sensible.
6. Test the map in real decisions. Have a seller or researcher use it to ask better questions. Update it when a buyer introduces an alternative you missed or a product changes materially.

## Worked example

A fictional procurement tool competes with a shared spreadsheet. The spreadsheet is inexpensive, familiar and easy to customize. The product's relevant difference is controlled approvals and a durable audit trail across departments, not simply “more automation.”

For a team handling five purchases a month with one approver, the spreadsheet may remain adequate. For a group with delegated budgets and recurring audit disputes, the control problem may justify evaluation. The company tests a real approval exception with the buyer instead of claiming that spreadsheets never work.


## Use the comparison responsibly

Keep pricing units and package scopes comparable. Do not present an outdated screenshot as a current limitation or treat absence from a feature list as proof of absence. Obtain approval for customer-specific examples before publishing them. Internally, track no-decision reasons separately from named-competitor losses: they call for different responses.
