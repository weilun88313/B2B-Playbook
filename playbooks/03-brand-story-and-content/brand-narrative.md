A brand narrative connects a meaningful change in the buyer's world with the problem the company exists to address and its point of view about a better response. It should remain useful across launches. It should also leave room for the buyer to disagree.

## Establish the argument before the story

Use this guide when product announcements feel disconnected or teams tell conflicting stories about why the company matters. Distinguish a durable narrative from a founder's biography, an individual customer case and a first-call sales pitch. Those formats can support the narrative without sharing the same structure.

## Build a defensible sequence

1. Name the change in concrete terms. Show evidence of what is happening for the audience. Avoid declaring that every company faces an existential crisis merely because your product is new.
2. Describe the tension that follows. Explain where the previous approach still works and where it becomes less useful. A fair description gives buyers a way to locate their own circumstances.
3. State the point of view. Make an argument that changes a decision: what should people prioritize, question or stop doing? “Innovation matters” is too broad to guide action.
4. Connect the company's role to the argument. Explain the capability, practice or choice that demonstrates the point of view. Do not make the company the hero of every customer's story.
5. Attach evidence and boundaries. Separate observed changes from forecasts, customer examples from composite illustrations, and present capability from roadmap intent.
6. Test it in several settings. Ask a buyer, seller and new employee to retell the argument. Notice what they omit or overstate. Build a short version and a longer explanation with the same underlying logic.

## Worked example

A fictional procurement company argues that decentralized purchasing requires visible decision ownership. It supports the change with customer workflow observations and explains why routing every purchase through one central person causes delays in those settings.

Its role is to make approval responsibility explicit, not to eliminate judgment. The narrative can support an article about delegation, a customer story about an exception and a product release about approval records. None needs to claim that all central procurement is obsolete.

## Narrative working draft

```text
What is changing for this audience:
Evidence and counterexample:
Where the old approach struggles:
Our point of view and its practical consequence:
What the buyer can do differently:
Our role, proof and limits:
Short explanation in the buyer's language:
Claims that require review before publication:
```

## Maintain the argument

Keep a source record and an owner for the narrative. Revisit it when the market assumption or product promise changes, not whenever a campaign needs a fresh headline. If the only way to make the story exciting is to remove its qualifications, the underlying argument needs more work.
