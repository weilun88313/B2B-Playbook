# Multichannel sequence

**Last reviewed:** 2026-08-29

A multichannel sequence coordinates email, phone, professional networks, partners, events, and useful assets around one account hypothesis. It is not the same message copied across more channels.

## Use this when

- Account research and the likely buying seat are complete.
- Each channel has a credible role in the buyer's context.
- The team can stop, suppress, and record responses across systems.

## Do not use this when

- More channels are being used to compensate for weak relevance.
- Channel rules, consent, identity, or suppression handling are unclear.
- The sequence cannot explain what new evidence each step adds.

## Sequence design

| Step | Purpose | Possible channel | New evidence or value | Stop when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Trigger hypothesis | Test relevance | Email, phone, LinkedIn | Dated account evidence | The premise is false |
| 2. Alternative angle | Test the current process | Email or call | Specific limitation or trade-off | The alternative is corrected |
| 3. Seat discovery | Find decision ownership | Call, referral, partner | Buying-committee question | The correct seat is known |
| 4. Useful artifact | Help evaluation | Email, direct mail, event | Checklist, comparison, benchmark | The recipient declines |
| 5. Close the loop | Stop responsibly | Original channel | Clear hypothesis and future trigger | No qualified signal appears |

Every step must add information. “Just following up” is not a strategy.

## Channel roles

- **Email:** concise evidence, an artifact, or a written next step.
- **Phone:** rapid premise testing, role discovery, and live objection context.
- **LinkedIn:** professional context, visible expertise, and a lower-friction interaction—not automated familiarity.
- **Partner / referral:** trusted access when the relationship is real and permission is appropriate.
- **Event:** a bounded reason and time to meet around a shared context.
- **Direct mail:** a high-cost, high-specificity asset for a small number of accounts.

## Operating method

1. Write the account hypothesis and desired decision.
2. Choose the minimum channels required to test it.
3. Assign one new evidence contribution to every step.
4. Define timing from buyer context, not arbitrary persistence.
5. Configure reply, referral, opt-out, bounce, and disqualification stops.
6. Keep one system of record for sequence status and next action.
7. Review account and seat quality before rewriting copy.

## Pre-flight checklist

- [ ] The account and seat pass qualification.
- [ ] Each channel has a distinct purpose.
- [ ] Every step adds evidence or value.
- [ ] Timing reflects a buyer trigger or reasonable review window.
- [ ] Identity, sender details, and channel ownership are clear.
- [ ] Replies and referrals stop inappropriate automated steps.
- [ ] Opt-outs and suppression requests propagate across tools.
- [ ] Domain, mailbox, phone, profile, and link behavior were tested.
- [ ] A final close-the-loop step exists.

## Metrics

| Metric | Diagnostic use |
|---|---|
| Qualified response rate | Accounts confirming problem, timing, seat, or next action |
| Channel contribution | Which channel introduced new evidence or created the qualified response |
| Seat progression | Accounts reaching a required buying seat |
| Stop-rule compliance | Sequences correctly stopped after replies, opt-outs, or disqualification |
| Meeting-to-qualified-opportunity rate | Whether the sequence reaches the right accounts and people |

Sent volume, touch count, and open rate are operating diagnostics—not business outcomes.

## Common mistakes

- Repeating the same message on email, phone, and LinkedIn.
- Automating a channel before defining its job.
- Continuing after the recipient corrected the premise.
- Measuring activity without account or seat progression.
- Running separate suppression lists in disconnected tools.
