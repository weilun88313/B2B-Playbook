Account research turns public, permitted evidence into a decision about whether to contact an account, which seat to approach, and what hypothesis to test.

## Use this when

- The account passes initial ICP filters.
- A message needs more than industry and job-title personalization.
- The team must explain why an account entered active outbound.

## Do not use this when

- Research is being used to rescue a disqualified account.
- The team collects private or irrelevant personal detail.
- More research will not change the tier, seat, message, or next action.

## Research order

1. **Account fit:** business job, operating context, scale, geography, and disqualifiers.
2. **Trigger:** a dated change, event, deadline, renewal, incident, hiring pattern, or strategic move.
3. **Current alternative:** the process, vendor, internal team, spreadsheet, or inaction used today.
4. **Buying seats:** the roles that own outcome, work, technical risk, and approval.
5. **Person:** only the professional context required to approach the relevant seat.

Research the account before the person. A highly personalized message to the wrong account is still wrong.

## Operating method

### Step 1: write the hypothesis before searching

> [Account] may fit because ___, and [trigger] may make [business job] important now. The likely current alternative is ___, owned by ___; the main unknown is ___.

This prevents browsing from turning into unbounded collection.

### How a filled hypothesis reads

> A ~400-employee B2B SaaS may fit because customer ops owns security questionnaires, and three security-engineer jobs this month may make pack turnaround urgent now. The likely current alternative is a spreadsheet plus a shared drive, owned by customer ops; the main unknown is who in security can stop a vendor.

Do not use a real account name you cannot source. The sentence shape is the artifact.

### Step 2: collect evidence with source and date

Useful sources may include company websites, filings, job posts, official announcements, event directories, product documentation, interviews, and permitted commercial data. A source proves only what it actually states.

### Step 3: classify each item

Teaching fill:

| Item | Source / date | Label | Decision impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three “security engineer” jobs | Careers page / this month | Fact | Trigger score 2; still not a budget |
| “We take security seriously” | Homepage / undated | Observation | Not a trigger |
| CISO will sign | None | Assumption | Do not write it into the email |
| Who ran the last questionnaire | — | To validate | Discovery action before send |

Copy: item / source-date / label / impact. Stop when you can pursue, test, pause, or disqualify.

### Step 4: decide whether the trigger is real

A trigger must change priority or timing. Generic growth, an undated technology claim, or presence in a database is not automatically a trigger.

### Step 5: map the likely buying seat

Use the [buying-committee playbook](../01-strategy-and-buyers/buying-committee.md). Write `unknown` when evidence does not support a seat.

### Step 6: stop research with a decision

Research ends when the team can pursue, test, pause, or disqualify the account. It does not end when every field is full.

## Pre-flight checklist

- [ ] The account passes hard ICP exclusions.
- [ ] Every fact has a source and date.
- [ ] The trigger changes priority or timing.
- [ ] The current alternative is named or marked unknown.
- [ ] The target person maps to a buying seat.
- [ ] No private or irrelevant personal detail is included.
- [ ] Unknowns are visible.
- [ ] The research produces a decision and stop condition.

## Metrics

| Metric | Diagnostic use |
|---|---|
| Premise confirmation rate | Replies or conversations confirming the trigger, alternative, or seat |
| Premise correction rate | Research hypotheses corrected by the market |
| Research-to-contact time | Detects excessive research that does not improve decisions |
| Disqualification yield | Accounts correctly removed before expensive outreach |
| Source error rate | Sampled facts that are stale, unsupported, or incorrectly interpreted |

## Common mistakes

- Treating a funding announcement as a universal buying trigger.
- Copying a person's recent post without connecting it to a business decision.
- Confusing database fields with verified facts.
- Researching indefinitely because there is no stop rule.
- Hiding uncertainty inside confident personalization.
