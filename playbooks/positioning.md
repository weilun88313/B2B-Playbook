# Positioning

Positioning is the shortest credible explanation of why a particular buyer
should change what they do now.

## The positioning sentence

Use this structure:

`For [specific buyer] facing [trigger], [category] helps them [outcome]
without [important trade-off].`

Then answer four questions:

1. What problem is expensive, urgent, or strategically important?
2. Who owns the problem and who experiences it?
3. What is the current alternative, including doing nothing?
4. What evidence makes the new choice believable?

The trigger should be observable: a deadline, renewal, event, hiring plan,
regulatory change, missed target, or operational failure. A feature is not
a trigger.

If the alternative cannot be named, the positioning is probably still
describing the product instead of the market.

## Ten-second homepage

A visitor who will never read the blog must see:

1. What this is, using category language buyers already use.
2. Who it is for, including role and team.
3. What it replaces or improves.
4. What outcome can be expected.
5. What the next step is.

Add the first constraint a skeptical buyer will check: security, data
source, deployment, integration, or implementation effort. If that line is
missing, the page is probably written for the seller.

## Contrast pages

Each competitor or alternative deserves a separate page only when the
comparison represents a real buying decision. The page should help a
champion explain the choice internally, with clear differences, trade-offs,
proof, and a fair account of where the alternative is stronger.
