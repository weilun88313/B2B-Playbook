Paid social puts an idea in front of people who may not be shopping now. Its first design decision is the audience's situation and the useful thought or action the creative can earn. Treating every impression as a request for a sales call produces misleading expectations.

## Choose the job

Use a campaign to build recognition, teach a consequential idea, or invite a relevant next step. Keep those jobs distinct in the scorecard. If the objective is immediate qualified demand, make the offer and expected follow-up explicit.

## Build a useful test

1. **Define audience eligibility.** Combine a credible business problem with observable targeting criteria. Document who the platform can actually reach, not just the ideal audience in the strategy deck. Check exclusions, geography, and permitted data use.
2. **Choose an offer appropriate to attention.** A short demonstration can work before a consultation request. A report download should deliver the report, not quietly promise a meeting the reader did not request.
3. **Develop distinct creative hypotheses.** Test the problem, mechanism, or proof angle. Changing only the background color rarely tests the underlying proposition. Keep each claim within its evidence.
4. **Match the destination.** Repeat the promise, remove unrelated navigation when appropriate, and show what happens after submission. Test native forms as an experience, including delivery and routing, rather than as a cheaper lead counter.
5. **Bound the experiment.** Set spend and time limits, define the unit of comparison, and avoid changing audience, offer, and creative simultaneously. Small samples should produce tentative conclusions.
6. **Review attention and progression separately.** Use reach and frequency to understand delivery; use appropriate downstream outcomes to evaluate the campaign's job. Inspect lead quality, duplicates, existing customers, and delayed outcomes before scaling.

## Worked example

A fictional security tool targets operations leaders opening new sites. One creative shows the access-removal problem; another demonstrates a review checklist. Both lead to the same ungated checklist with an optional implementation consultation.

The checklist creative earns more clicks, but the problem creative yields more relevant consultations. The team retains the checklist for education and investigates the consultation difference before reallocating the acquisition budget. It does not declare one universal winner from two different audience behaviors.

Add actual spend, reach, frequency, valid responses, and the reason for the next decision. Preserve the creative version so a later report refers to the same asset.

## Watch for false success

High click rates can reflect curiosity, ambiguity, or accidental taps. Low delivery may reflect a very narrow audience rather than a weak idea. Investigate those causes before rewriting the offer or expanding eligibility indiscriminately.
