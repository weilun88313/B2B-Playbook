# Enterprise sales SOP

CRM stages forecast probability; they do not describe the buyer's process. The seller's job is to mirror and responsibly coordinate how the customer will decide.

Operating principles:

1. Enter through the executive or their direct report. Lower-level access can teach user value but rarely preserves the executive story.
2. Treat the demo as leverage, not the first step. Use it to earn the right people and the right decision frame.
3. Slow down early to reduce late rework. Several short meetings can compress the overall cycle.
4. Run sales as project management plus narrative discipline.

This module adapts practices discussed in the public [Lenny's Podcast conversation with Jen Abel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YS9In813jJ0). Any numeric range below is a hypothesis to validate in your own funnel, not a performance promise.

## 1. Earn the first meeting

The preferred entry seats are the executive who owns the outcome and the direct report likely to become the champion.

- A founder can approach the executive while an enterprise seller approaches the direct report.
- When one side responds, use role parity to bring the other side into the process.
- Keep initial outreach short and centered on the strategic outcome the buyer can defend internally.
- If the message cannot interest the executive without a feature list, the executive motion is not ready.

See [03 · Outbound](../03-outbound/).

## 2. Run the informal first conversation

The first conversation carries the most information because the buyer usually becomes more guarded later.

- Do not begin with a demo or deck.
- Avoid recording when a candid exploratory conversation is more valuable and policy allows it; never record without proper consent.
- Let the buyer explain the context before the seller frames the product.
- Ask what must be different next year and why it cannot wait another year.
- Explore how the outcome is measured and who feels the consequence.
- Explain the product only after the frame is clear, then keep asking.
- Hold budget, authority, need, and timing in the seller's head rather than reading BANT questions aloud.
- Be willing to stop when the organization is not mature enough to change.

Use the [intro-call guide](templates/intro-call-guide.md) and [checklist](checklists/intro-call.md).

## 3. Co-design before the formal demo

Do not schedule a broad demo immediately after discovery. Ask the champion for a short working session:

> Before the demo, I need to confirm who should be in the room and which part of the workflow will matter to them.

Walk the champion through the possible product areas and let them select the two that will resonate internally. Ask what each attendee cares about, what to avoid, and which question the champion should raise. If another seat can provide meaningful intelligence, run a short pre-demo.

Use the [demo brief](templates/demo-brief.md).

## 4. Run a decision-specific demo

- The room should include the people who will shape approval and implementation.
- Re-open in the customer's frame for attendees who missed earlier conversations.
- Ask new attendees what they need from the session.
- Demonstrate the smallest part of the product that proves the agreed outcome.
- Park adjacent requests for a separate working session rather than expanding into a generic product tour.
- Decline checklist demos that include many reviewers but provide no decision context.

Run the [demo-readiness checklist](checklists/demo-readiness.md).

## 5. Debrief immediately

Contact the champion immediately after the group session, before internal opinions harden:

- What landed?
- Who was missing or unconvinced?
- Which objection needs a separate conversation?
- Did the decision process change?

Use the existing evidence to request a focused follow-up rather than sending “still interested?” messages.

## 6. Discuss pricing with the champion

Avoid a fixed price before the buyer understands the scope and value. In a one-to-one conversation with the champion:

- Ask how they would defend the purchase internally.
- Let the buyer reveal the number they can responsibly carry before offering a discount.
- Separate scope, implementation, term, and commercial timing.
- When appropriate, use a lower year-one deployment scope with a committed path to full value.
- If forced to discuss price during a group demo, provide a clearly bounded range and its assumptions.

Use [pricing-with-champion.md](templates/pricing-with-champion.md).

## 7. Design the pilot backward from signature

Before opening a pilot, ask: if this succeeds, when can the customer sign, and which security, legal, procurement, or implementation seats must be involved now?

If a successful pilot still leads to a quarter of waiting, delay the pilot. Momentum will decay.

| Pilot type | Default design |
|---|---|
| Value can be experienced without customer integration | Two to three days with a narrow task set |
| Integration or configuration is required | One to two months, paid when appropriate, with the fee credited after purchase |

Rules:

- Use three to four real operators; executives are usually sponsors, not users.
- Keep the champion inside the pilot loop.
- Co-write three concrete tasks and observable success conditions.
- Remove setup work so users reach the meaningful action quickly.
- Keep the implementation effort proportional to the deal.

Use the [pilot template](templates/pilot-success.md) and [checklist](checklists/pilot.md).

## 8. Review actual pilot behavior

Compare agreed tasks with completed tasks and observed outcomes. A login is not evidence of value. When a user stops at the first step, investigate the experience with the champion rather than treating the user as the problem.

A silent champion is a risk signal. A real champion normally explains when the deal must pause.

## 9. Coordinate procurement, legal, and signature

Business enthusiasm does not authorize payment. Do not begin production work before paper is signed.

- Tie commercial conditions to real dates and delivery capacity, not fake urgency.
- Send an editable agreement when redlines are expected.
- Ask whether the buyer will use seller paper or buyer paper.
- Resolve simple redlines quickly; move material legal disagreements into a live counsel-to-counsel conversation.
- Confirm the actual signatory before routing the agreement.
- Give every procurement, security, legal, and finance action an owner and date.

Run the [papering checklist](checklists/papering.md).

## 10. Plan expansion after signature

Enterprise economics depend on durable adoption and expansion. Early customers also teach which implementation work should become product and which should remain a paid service. Record the next expansion hypothesis without turning it into an unapproved promise.

## Exit criteria

- Entry came through an executive or direct report and the story describes a business outcome.
- At least one co-design conversation happened before the group demo.
- Pricing was reviewed with the champion.
- The pilot had co-written success criteria and a signature timeline.
- Papering has named owners and dates; production work did not begin before signature.
