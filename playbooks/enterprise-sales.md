# Enterprise sales

CRM stages forecast probability; they do not describe how a buyer decides. Enterprise selling is project management plus narrative discipline across executive access, discovery, demo co-design, pricing, pilot design, procurement, paper, and signature.

This playbook adapts practices discussed in the public [Lenny's Podcast conversation with Jen Abel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YS9In813jJ0). Numeric ranges are hypotheses to validate in your own funnel, not performance promises.

## Use this when

- The deal is valuable and complex enough to require executive approval.
- An executive or direct report can enter the process.
- Security, implementation, pilot, procurement, or legal review shapes the decision.

## Do not use this when

- Discovery is a scripted BANT interview.
- The seller demos before understanding the decision.
- The deal cannot support a multi-meeting buying process.
- Production work is being used to bypass qualification or signature.

## Operating principles

1. Enter through the executive or the direct report likely to become the champion.
2. Treat the demo as leverage, not the first step.
3. Slow down early to reduce late rework.
4. Mirror the buyer's process instead of forcing it into CRM labels.
5. Give every material action an owner and date.

## Operating method

### Step 1: earn the first meeting

The preferred entry seats are the executive who owns the outcome and the direct report likely to become the champion.

- A founder can approach the executive while an enterprise seller approaches the direct report.
- When one side responds, use role parity to bring the other into the process.
- Center the initial story on the strategic outcome the buyer can defend internally.
- If the story cannot interest the executive without a feature list, the executive motion is not ready.

Use the [outbound playbook](outbound.md) for account research and first contact.

### Step 2: run the informal first conversation

The first conversation often carries the most information because the buyer becomes more guarded later.

- Do not begin with a demo or deck.
- Let the buyer explain context before framing the product.
- Ask what must be different next year and why it cannot wait.
- Explore how the outcome is measured and who feels the consequence.
- Name the current alternative and missing committee seats.
- Explain the product only after the frame is clear.
- Be willing to stop when the organization is not ready to change.
- Recording and consent must follow policy and applicable rules.

### Step 3: co-design the formal demo

Ask the champion for a short working session:

> Before the demo, I need to confirm who should be in the room and which part of the workflow will matter to them.

Let the champion select the two or three product areas that will resonate internally. Ask what each attendee cares about, what to avoid, and which question the champion should raise. Run a short pre-demo with another seat when it will prevent the group meeting from being derailed.

### Step 4: run a decision-specific demo

- Include the people who shape approval and implementation.
- Re-open in the customer's frame for new attendees.
- Ask new attendees what they need from the session.
- Demonstrate the smallest product surface that proves the agreed outcome.
- Park adjacent requests for another working session.
- Give the champion an active role.
- Decline checklist demos that provide no decision context.

### Step 5: debrief immediately

Contact the champion before internal opinions harden:

- What landed?
- Who was missing or unconvinced?
- Which objection needs a separate conversation?
- Did the decision process change?

Request a focused follow-up from the evidence instead of sending “still interested?” messages.

### Step 6: discuss pricing with the champion

- Ask how the champion would defend the purchase internally.
- Let the buyer name the constraint before offering a discount.
- Separate scope, implementation, term, confidence, and timing.
- Consider a lower year-one deployment scope with a path to the preferred outcome.
- If price must be discussed in a group, give a bounded range and assumptions.

### Step 7: design the pilot backward from signature

Before opening a pilot, ask: if it succeeds, when can the buyer sign, and which security, legal, procurement, implementation, and signatory seats must participate now?

| Pilot type | Default design |
|---|---|
| Value can be experienced without integration | Two to three days with a narrow task set |
| Integration or configuration is required | One to two months, paid when appropriate, with a defined commercial path |

Rules:

- Use three to four real operators; executives are usually sponsors, not users.
- Keep the champion inside the pilot loop.
- Co-write three concrete tasks and observable success conditions.
- Remove setup work so users reach the meaningful action quickly.
- Keep implementation proportional to the deal.
- Define a stop condition.

### Step 8: review actual behavior

Compare agreed tasks with completed tasks and observed outcomes. A login is not evidence of value. When a user stops at the first step, investigate the experience with the champion instead of blaming the user.

A silent champion is a risk signal. A working champion usually explains when and why the deal must pause.

### Step 9: coordinate procurement, legal, and signature

Business enthusiasm does not authorize payment or production access.

- Tie commercial conditions to real dates and capacity, not fake urgency.
- Send an editable agreement when redlines are expected.
- Ask whether the buyer will use seller paper or buyer paper.
- Move material legal disagreements into a qualified owner-to-owner conversation.
- Confirm the actual signatory before routing.
- Give every procurement, security, finance, implementation, and legal action an owner and date.
- Do not begin production work or use customer data before the required authorization and signature.

### Step 10: plan expansion after signature

Record the next expansion hypothesis without turning it into an unapproved promise. Early deals should reveal which implementation work belongs in the product and which remains a service.

## Checklists

### Intro call

- [ ] The attendee is an executive, direct report, or useful discovery seat.
- [ ] The seller can state the trigger and account hypothesis.
- [ ] The buyer speaks before the product story begins.
- [ ] The current alternative and missing seats are recorded.
- [ ] No demo is promised before the room and scope are understood.
- [ ] The next step tests maturity instead of assuming a deal.

### Demo readiness

- [ ] The champion helped choose the scope.
- [ ] Every invited seat has a reason to attend.
- [ ] The opening uses the customer's outcome and language.
- [ ] Topics to prove and topics to park are explicit.
- [ ] The objection or unknown the demo must resolve is named.
- [ ] The champion has an active role.
- [ ] A post-demo debrief is planned.

### Pilot

- [ ] The buyer stated what happens and when if the pilot succeeds.
- [ ] Required decision-process seats are identified.
- [ ] The champion co-wrote success criteria.
- [ ] Three concrete user tasks are defined.
- [ ] Appropriate operators are committed.
- [ ] Setup work is minimized and owned.
- [ ] Task completion—not logins—drives review.
- [ ] A stop condition exists.

### Papering

- [ ] The business buyer confirmed intent and scope.
- [ ] The actual signatory is named.
- [ ] Procurement, legal, security, finance, and implementation owners are named.
- [ ] Seller paper versus buyer paper is agreed.
- [ ] Material redlines have a qualified resolution path.
- [ ] Every outstanding action has an owner and date.
- [ ] No production work or customer-data access begins before authorization and signature.

## Metrics

| Signal | Diagnostic question when weak |
|---|---|
| Right seat → qualified opportunity | Is the entry seat wrong, or is the story too tactical? |
| Discovery → co-designed demo | Is the seller showing the product before earning context? |
| Demo → pilot or commercial process | Did the demo prove a narrow decision or expose irrelevant product surface? |
| Pilot → next buyer action | Was success and timing agreed before the pilot? |
| Qualified opportunity → signature | Is qualification loose, pricing misaligned, or the decision process unmanaged? |

Also track account-specific demo scope, production work before signature (target: zero), and opportunities with named procurement, legal, security, and signatory owners.

Do not count CRM-stage movement, demo volume, or pilot logins as buyer progress.
